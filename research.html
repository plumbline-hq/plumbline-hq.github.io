Research

Measurements of the agent economy, done the way we do client work: reproducible, with the method published and the uncomfortable results kept in.

Every number here was derived from public data by tooling we will hand you. Where we could not verify something, we say so — including when the unverifiable claim was our own.

The x402 economy is smaller than reported, and shrinking

A widely-shared post measured the independent x402 seller economy at $11,748/month (measured 2026-08-07). We re-derived it from the same public catalogue rather than taking the figure on trust, and got $8,665.76/month on 2026-09-01 across 14,656 listed resources.

Three snapshots, two independent parties, same direction. If you are building a business on agent-to-agent payment volume today, this is the size of the pond.

The eleven-orders-of-magnitude landmine

The naive version of this measurement — take the first payment option each listing offers, assume 6-decimal USDC — produces $610 billion per month. The entire error comes from one listing whose first offer is priced in an 18-decimal token: read naively, a single call appears to cost $10 billion. The same listing's USDC option, further down the array, prices it at $0.01.

We mention this because it is the whole argument for what we do. The data was public, the code was simple, and the answer was wrong by eleven orders of magnitude — because of an assumption nobody stated. Our figure only prices from (network, asset) pairs with known decimals and excludes implausible outliers rather than trusting the first offer in the list.

18% is the floor, not the estimate. It counts only failures visible without paying. Settlement failures are invisible to inspection — we know because we caught our own endpoint serving a flawless payment challenge while being structurally unable to collect. It would have passed this survey.

15% of x402 sellers have a paywall a HEAD request walks through

That one-shot sample is now a standing survey. As of 2026-09-05 we hold current, same-version inspections of 651 catalogued endpoints across 62 hosts — every one of them listed by the directory as healthy. The single largest defect is not a dead route. It is a paywall keyed to the HTTP verb:

FailureEndpointsHostsWhat it means
Paywall bypassed by HEAD1799GET returns 402; HEAD returns 200
Unusable payment requirement1834402 an agent cannot pay from
Unreachable14010listed as live; is not

9 of the 62 hosts we track — 15% — answer HEAD with a 200 on a route that answers GET with a 402. On 2026-09-02 we re-ran four of them by hand, on four different operators: 402 to GET, 200 to HEAD, four for four. Widening the corpus since has not moved the host count: the same 9 operators, now over more of their routes. Where the response carries a content-length or an ETag, the seller has also handed a non-payer a free change-oracle on the paid artifact. We checked our own four paid routes against the same probe before publishing this: all four answer 402 to HEAD as well as GET.

Why we are not publishing the 56%

367 of those 651 endpoints fail. A third of the market is a better headline than anything else on this page, and we are not running it, because the corpus does not support it. Four hosts supply 62% of the endpoints. One operator (clonecho.builda.company) supplies 41% of the failures across its 1 deployments, and 150 of the 183 unusable-requirement failures. "A third of the x402 market is broken" would have been one seller's bug wearing a market's clothes.

Host-weighted, the number we will stand behind is 22 of 62 hosts (35%) have at least one failing endpoint, with each finding carried alongside the number of operators it spans — which is why the table above has a Hosts column. The distinction is the whole result: 179 endpoints on 9 hosts is a market finding, 150 of 183 on one operator's two deployments is a bug report.

This is enforced in the tool, not in our good intentions. observatory-stats.py now computes every rate host-weighted as well as endpoint-weighted, and stamps the endpoint-weighted rate NOT PUBLISHABLE when a single host supplies a third or more of the corpus or of the failures. It refused this one. Every figure in this section carries the key that derives it (data-fig); scripts/site-figures.py recomputes each one from the observation ledger and fails if the page has drifted from it.

llms.txt is barely read — including by us, until we checked

Our audit used to flag a missing llms.txt as a warning. Then we found a study of 83 sites over 12 weeks measuring what AI crawlers actually fetch:

We verified those numbers against the primary source, then downgraded our own check — against our commercial interest, since "your site fails" sells better than "this is cheap insurance." robots.txt is where AI access is actually decided today, so that is what our audit now weighs.

Follow-up: we went looking for independent corroboration at larger scale, and found it. Ahrefs analysed 137,210 domains in May 2026 and found 97% of published llms.txt files got zero requests all month. Same direction as the 83-site study, two orders of magnitude more sites.

Correction, 2026-09-01: an earlier version of this paragraph said "AI training crawlers and assistants combined were a fifth" of the traffic the other 3% did get. That mislabels the bucket, and it overstated AI interest in the file, so we are fixing it against the direction that flatters us. The fifth (19.5%) is all AI bot categories together: user-acting agents 10.5%, training crawlers 5.3%, assistants 2.5%, retrieval bots 1.1%. The largest single category of any kind is SEO audit tools at 21.7% — tools like ours, checking whether the file exists.

Two details from that breakdown changed how we read the whole thing. Requests to a missing llms.txt were 98% human (SEOs checking competitors) with a zero AI-bot share — nothing in 137,210 domains speculatively probed for a file that wasn't there, so not publishing one costs no measured AI traffic today. But the biggest AI reader of the files that are published is an agent acting on a user's behalf (Ahrefs' example: Claude‑Code), not a training crawler or a search assistant. llms.txt is not a search-visibility channel; it is weakly, measurably an agent-execution one. That is why our check says INFO rather than nothing at all. Ahrefs' caveat, which bounds every number above and applies to our own existence check too: "'fetched' doesn't mean 'read'."

Method

Everything above is read-only against public data: an identifying user-agent, no authentication attempts, no fuzzing, and no payments made to third-party endpoints. Where a claim could only be settled by paying someone, we marked it unverified rather than guessing. The standing survey costs each catalogued endpoint one GET and one HEAD per visit — the HEAD is what detects the verb bypass, and on a correctly gated endpoint it costs the seller a 402. Rates are computed only within a single checker version, because a failure rate blended across two versions of the checker is two instruments averaged, and they are reported host-weighted as well as endpoint-weighted, because a catalogue counts endpoints while a market is made of operators.
